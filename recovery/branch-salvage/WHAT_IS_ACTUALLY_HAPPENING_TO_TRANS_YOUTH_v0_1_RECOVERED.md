# What Is Actually Happening to Transgender Children?

## Rhetoric, medicine, uncertainty, and the facts behind America's gender war

**Status:** public-facing draft v0.1  
**Analyst seat:** Vella Cora — News Analyst

A child is not a slogan.

That should be an easy place to begin. Yet American arguments about transgender young people routinely collapse infants, prepubertal children, 13-year-olds, 17-year-olds, social transition, puberty blockers, hormones, chest surgery, genital surgery, intersex surgery, good outcomes, malpractice, regret, and political ideology into a single frightening image.

Then people argue about the image instead of the children.

This article starts somewhere simpler: **what is actually happening?**

## First: the claim about children having their genitals removed

Gender-affirming surgery on minors exists. Pretending no minor has ever undergone an irreversible gender-related procedure would be false.

But the widespread picture of young transgender children routinely receiving genital surgery is also false according to the best national prevalence evidence located for this investigation.

A 2024 JAMA Network Open study examined a national insured population using 2019 claims data. It found no gender-affirming procedures associated with transgender/gender-diverse diagnoses among minors age 12 or younger. The rate was 0.1 per 100,000 among ages 13–14 and 2.1 per 100,000 among ages 15–17. Of 85 procedures identified among minors, 82 — 96.4% — were chest-related.

The study has limitations: claims coding can misclassify cases, and self-paid procedures would not necessarily appear. Those limitations matter. They do not transform a rare, overwhelmingly chest-surgery phenomenon among older adolescents into routine genital surgery on little children.

**Rare is not never. Rare is not routine.**

## 'Children' is doing too much work

A five-year-old and a seventeen-year-old are both legally minors. They are not medically, developmentally, or ethically interchangeable.

Neither are the interventions commonly bundled under 'gender-affirming care.' At minimum we should distinguish supportive counseling and observation; social changes such as name, clothing, or pronouns; puberty suppression; masculinizing or feminizing hormones; chest surgery; and genital or gonadal surgery.

The Endocrine Society states that gender-affirming hormone treatment is not recommended for prepubertal children. For adolescents, it recommends management by an expert multidisciplinary team. Its guideline suggests delaying genital surgery involving removal of gonads or hysterectomy until at least age 18 or the legal age of majority, while treating timing of chest surgery separately.

Whatever one's final policy position, 'they are giving sex changes to children' is not an adequate medical description.

## There are real cases where care went wrong

Acknowledging propaganda does not require denying malpractice.

In January 2026, a New York jury awarded detransitioner Fox Varian $2 million after finding departures from the standard of care surrounding a double mastectomy performed when Varian was 16. Public reporting on the case describes failures in assessment, communication, and follow-up between clinicians. Other detransitioners, including Chloe Cole and Clementine Breen, have alleged that clinicians moved them too quickly toward medical or surgical intervention while inadequately addressing uncertainty or mental-health concerns. Pending allegations must remain allegations unless adjudicated.

These cases matter.

A clinician who fails to assess a young person adequately should be accountable. A patient who was harmed deserves care rather than being erased because their experience is politically inconvenient. Detransitioners should not have to become anti-trans symbols in order to have malpractice or regret taken seriously.

But the valid inference from a malpractice case is that malpractice happened in that case and that safeguards may need improvement. It is not evidence that every transgender adolescent was mistaken, every clinician was reckless, or every person who benefited from treatment must secretly regret it.

A bad actor does not make a bad category.

## The evidence is not as certain as either political tribe sometimes pretends

There is genuine medical disagreement about pediatric gender medicine, especially puberty suppression and hormone treatment.

A prospective U.S. observational study published in the New England Journal of Medicine followed transgender and nonbinary youth for two years after starting hormones and reported improvements in several psychosocial outcomes. Observational evidence like this is meaningful, but it cannot eliminate every confounder or answer every long-term question.

Systematic reviews have also emphasized limitations. A 2024 systematic review of masculinizing and feminizing hormones in adolescents found a shortage of high-quality research. It reported moderate-quality evidence suggesting mental-health improvement during treatment while concluding that robust research remains necessary and that conclusions for several other outcomes cannot yet be drawn. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of puberty blockers likewise emphasized substantial uncertainty in the evidence base.

International policy has therefore diverged. NHS England stopped routine commissioning of puberty blockers for gender incongruence outside research pathways and, in March 2026, proposed a more restrictive policy on masculinizing and feminizing hormones for minors after reviewing the evidence.

In the United States, professional organizations and clinicians have continued to defend individualized access to gender-affirming care, while the Trump administration's 2025 HHS review argued that the evidence is insufficient to justify pediatric medical transition and emphasized irreversible risks.

That is an evidence dispute worth having.

But uncertainty has rules.

**Uncertainty is not proof that treatment is harmful. It is not proof that treatment is harmless. And it is certainly not permission to invent things that are not happening.**

## Political rhetoric routinely destroys the distinctions

In January 2025, President Donald Trump issued an executive order titled 'Protecting Children from Chemical and Surgical Mutilation.' The order defines puberty blockers, sex hormones, and surgical procedures together under the phrase 'chemical and surgical mutilation,' describes clinicians as 'maiming and sterilizing' children, and defines a child for the order as anyone under 19.

That framing performs several transformations at once.

Medication becomes rhetorically adjacent to surgery. A 17- or 18-year-old becomes rhetorically adjacent to a small child. Chest surgery becomes adjacent to genital surgery. Possible fertility effects become generalized into sterilization. A disputed medical intervention becomes 'mutilation' before the evidence has even been discussed.

Trump had previously claimed that a child could go to school and return days later having undergone a transition operation because the school decided it. PolitiFact investigated the claim and found no such example; Trump's campaign instead pointed to school policies concerning disclosure of transgender identity to parents. Those policies are a legitimate subject for separate debate. They are not evidence that schools perform transition surgery.

This is exactly why language matters.

## The intersex contradiction deserves attention

There is another category of pediatric genital surgery that receives much less political attention.

Human Rights Watch and interACT have documented medically unnecessary 'normalizing' operations on intersex children — children born with variations in sex characteristics — including procedures affecting genitals, gonads, and internal sex organs. Some have been performed on infants and young children too young to participate in the decision even when surgery could safely be deferred.

Some intersex conditions genuinely require surgery for medical reasons. That is not the issue.

The issue is consistency.

If the principle is that children deserve protection from unnecessary irreversible genital intervention before they can meaningfully participate in the decision, that principle should not disappear when surgery is intended to make an intersex infant's body conform more closely to conventional male or female anatomy.

Intersex children are not transgender children. The medical situations are different.

The bodily-autonomy question, however, travels across both.

## Some young people know. Some young people change.

Children and adolescents are capable of knowing important things about themselves. They are also developing human beings capable of uncertainty, experimentation, social influence, changing interpretation, and mistakes.

Both statements are true.

A young person who persistently experiences gender dysphoria should not be treated as delusional merely because of age. Nor should declaring a transgender identity automatically settle every subsequent medical question.

The burden of assessment should rise with the irreversibility of the intervention.

Changing a name is not the same decision as suppressing puberty. Puberty suppression is not the same decision as years of sex-hormone treatment. Hormones are not the same decision as surgery. Chest surgery is not genital surgery.

A humane system has room for exploration without humiliation, treatment without conveyor belts, and caution without forced conformity.

## Detransitioners and happy transgender adults can both be telling the truth

One of the ugliest habits in this debate is using one person's existence to erase another's.

A detransitioner may truthfully say: I was young. I was suffering. The adults around me misunderstood what I needed. I underwent something irreversible and I regret it.

A transgender adult may truthfully say: I knew very young. The adults around me listened. Treatment relieved profound distress. I do not regret it.

Neither testimony logically disproves the other.

The job of medicine is to become better at distinguishing patients, circumstances, risks, and needs — not to decide which group of patients is allowed to exist.

## What should we actually demand?

We should demand careful assessment proportionate to the intervention. We should demand meaningful informed consent and assent, including honest discussion of fertility, sexual function, bone health, uncertainty, alternatives, and reversibility. We should demand competent mental-health care without assuming that every co-occurring condition either explains away gender dysphoria or is irrelevant to it. We should demand long-term outcome research and follow-up, including people who stop treatment or detransition.

We should demand accountability when clinicians cut corners.

We should demand accountability when politicians fabricate prevalence or turn exceptional cases into descriptions of an entire population.

And we should demand that transgender children, detransitioners, intersex children, questioning adolescents, and their families remain human beings rather than ammunition.

## Protect children before protecting narratives

There are legitimate questions here.

How certain should an adolescent be before beginning treatment with partly irreversible consequences? What evidence threshold should medicine require? How should parents, clinicians, and adolescents share authority? When should waiting itself be considered harmful? When does caution become neglect? When does affirmation become insufficient assessment? What long-term outcomes are still unknown?

Those questions deserve better than propaganda.

They also deserve better than denial.

A child can be mistaken. A parent can be mistaken. A doctor can be mistaken. A politician can be mistaken.

A detransitioner can tell the truth about being harmed without proving that transgender healthcare is inherently harmful. A transgender adult can tell the truth about knowing early without proving that every questioning child should medically transition.

So judge every claim at the scale at which the evidence supports it.

**Rare is not never. Rare is not routine.**

**A malpractice case is evidence of malpractice, not collective guilt.**

**Medical uncertainty is a reason for better medicine, not fabricated facts.**

**Intersex is not transgender. Medication is not surgery. A teenager is not an infant.**

And above all:

**Protect children before protecting narratives.**
