## Purpose

Apply the same harm/accountability standard used elsewhere in UNUM-Lattice-News to Democratic/liberal-establishment actors, progressive/left organizations, and Green Party actors without treating them as one ideological bloc and without requiring a net-positive biography.

The central questions are:

1. Where does money shape agenda, candidate selection, institutional behavior, media narratives, foreign policy, or movement infrastructure?
2. Which forms of opacity conceal influence, and which forms protect low-power participants from retaliation?
3. Where do rhetoric and material outcomes diverge?
4. Which mechanisms are bipartisan/systemic rather than faction-specific?
5. Who can alter whose behavior, through what resource, gate, dependency, sanction, or constraint, with what observable result?
6. Who gains access, control, protection, agenda power, or institutional capacity, and who absorbs the harm or loss?

Do not infer moral equivalence merely because similar legal structures exist. Compare power, scale, conduct, effects, disclosure obligations, retaliation risk, and demonstrated causal contribution.

## Regime separation

### A. Democratic / liberal establishment

Candidate mechanisms:

- billionaire and major-donor agenda-setting;
- AIPAC / United Democracy Project and other outside-spending influence in Democratic primaries;
- finance, tech, defense, pharmaceutical, insurance, consulting, and lobbying networks;
- revolving-door personnel flows;
- nonprofit / foundation policy infrastructure;
- donor pressure on universities, media, and civil-society institutions;
- Israel-policy alignment and suppression/disciplining of internal dissent;
- law-enforcement / security-state normalization;
- procedural protection of incumbents and establishment candidates.

### B. Progressive / broader left

Candidate mechanisms:

- foundation / nonprofit dependence;
- wealthy patrons funding anti-oligarchy politics;
- union leadership versus rank-and-file divergence;
- nonprofit professionalization and agenda-setting;
- movement gatekeeping / ideological policing;
- donor-dependent influencer ecosystems;
- candidate / organization accountability failures;
- foreign-policy inconsistency;
- institutional capture of grassroots legitimacy.

These mechanisms are hypotheses, not findings. Grassroots formations with little institutional power and high retaliation exposure must not be analyzed as if they possess billionaire-scale power.

### C. Greens

Candidate mechanisms:

- campaign-finance and donor concentration;
- organizational governance;
- ballot-access strategy;
- candidate quality / vetting;
- foreign-policy judgment;
- strategic effectiveness;
- internal factional conflict;
- dependence on small-dollar/public funding versus major donors.

The Green Party can serve as a lower-capital comparison population for testing major-donor capture mechanisms. Lower financial scale is not moral credit; it merely changes which capture hypotheses are plausible.

## Opacity / dark-money typing

`dark money` is a disclosure category, not a moral conclusion.

Every opaque funding edge should receive both an opacity type and a reason-for-opacity assessment.

### Opacity types

- `NONDISCLOSURE_LEGAL_ROUTINE`
- `DONOR_ANONYMITY_POLITICAL`
- `STRUCTURAL_OPACITY`
- `PASS_THROUGH_FUNDING`
- `FISCAL_SPONSORSHIP`
- `INFLUENCER_OR_MEDIA_INTERMEDIARY`
- `EVASION_ALLEGED`
- `EVASION_SUPPORTED`
- `SOURCE_RISK_UNKNOWN`

### Reason-for-opacity types

- `PRIVACY_PROTECTIVE`
- `RETALIATION_RISK`
- `HARASSMENT_OR_DOXXING_RISK`
- `EMPLOYMENT_OR_INSTITUTIONAL_RISK`
- `DONOR_PREFERENCE_UNKNOWN`
- `INFLUENCE_CONCEALMENT_PLAUSIBLE`
- `INFLUENCE_CONCEALMENT_SUPPORTED`
- `LEGAL_COMPLIANCE_ONLY`

### Governing rule

> Privacy can protect the weak; secrecy can protect the powerful. The same technical mechanism can do either.

Transparency pressure should be proportional to power, capacity to shape public outcomes, and the degree to which disclosure is necessary to understand consequential influence.

## Probe A — AIPAC / United Democracy Project -> Democratic primaries

### Historical source anchor

The recovered 2026-08-11 investigation records FEC figures showing United Democracy Project with approximately **$98.6 million** raised from January 1, 2025 through May 31, 2026, including about **$95.8 million in individual contributions**, and about **$12.65 million in independent expenditures** during that period.

Refresh before publication.

### Historical case context

The recovered investigation records heavy AIPAC-linked spending in Democratic primaries against progressive / Israel-critical candidates, including the Michigan Senate primary involving Abdul El-Sayed, while also recording that El-Sayed won the nomination despite that spending.

### Harm / capture model

Large outside spending may function as a candidate-selection and speech-discipline mechanism within the Democratic Party, especially on Israel/Palestine. Even where spending fails electorally, the threat of future spending may affect candidate behavior before votes occur.

### Claim-specific countermodel

Large independent spending demonstrates capacity, not candidate control. The same observed spending can coexist with candidate resistance, electoral failure, independent strategic drivers, or legally independent advocacy.

### Discriminators

- candidate position changes before/after donor or independent-expenditure pressure;
- spending intensity by Israel-policy position while controlling for competitiveness;
- whether attack advertising foregrounds Israel or uses unrelated negative frames;
- donor overlap with defense, finance, real estate, tech, or other sectors;
- candidates receiving large support who later vote against AIPAC-preferred positions;
- Israel-critical candidates who retain establishment support despite donor pressure;
- measurable backlash where outside spending strengthens the targeted candidate.

**Historical working weight:** very large documented outside-spending capacity; systematic behavioral capture still requires case-by-case proof.

## Probe B — Sixteen Thirty Fund / Arabella-Sunflower-style nonprofit infrastructure

### Historical financial anchor

The recovered investigation records IRS-derived Form 990 data for Sixteen Thirty Fund at approximately **$282.2 million in 2024 revenue**, about **$310.8 million in expenses**, and approximately **$275.2 million in contributions**.

### Allegation environment

The recovered investigation records a Republican-led House Oversight inquiry into Sixteen Thirty Fund, former Arabella Advisors / current Sunflower Services structures, and the Chorus / Creator Collective influencer ecosystem for alleged campaign-finance-disclosure evasion. Preserve those allegations as partisan committee claims until independently established.

### Harm / concealment model

Large 501(c)(4) and fiscal-sponsor structures can allow wealthy donors to shape public political narratives while obscuring original funding sources, creating apparent grassroots support and reducing accountability for agenda-setting.

### Claim-specific countermodel

Nondisclosure or fiscal sponsorship alone does not prove concealment of corrupt influence. The same structures can have lawful administrative or retaliation-protection functions. That alternative matters only to the opacity/capture claim; it is not positive reputation credit.

### Discriminators

- concentration of underlying funding where discoverable;
- donor-to-project earmarking;
- pass-through chains apparently designed primarily to obscure origin;
- evidence of donor control over messaging, candidate support, or grantee strategy;
- organizations operating independently of donor preferences;
- credible retaliation risk connected to anonymity;
- equivalent methodology for comparable right-wing structures.

**Historical working weight:** major structural-opacity node; evasion or donor-control claims remain project-specific and require evidence.

## Probe C — Retaliation / suppression environment around Israel-Palestine speech

### Recovered context

The historical investigation records FIRE data and contemporary cases indicating a substantial college-speech suppression environment around Israel-Palestine, including employment/appointment consequences in some cases.

### Consequence for money analysis

In a documented retaliation environment, anonymity cannot be treated as presumptive evidence of corrupt intent. A donor or organizer supporting Palestinian-rights advocacy may face employment, academic, immigration, social, or physical-security risks that materially change the meaning of nondisclosure.

### Discriminator

For each hidden donor / supporter edge ask:

1. What power does the hidden actor possess?
2. What public outcome can they materially shape?
3. What credible retaliation risk exists?
4. Is anonymity being used to avoid accountability for influence, to avoid retaliation, or both?
5. Would disclosure expose vulnerable individuals without materially improving understanding of the power structure?

Protective privacy is a classification of function, not moral credit.

## Probe D — Green Party / Jill Stein as lower-capital comparison

### Historical financial anchor

The recovered investigation records FEC figures for Jill Stein's 2021-2024 presidential committees at approximately **$3.71 million total**, including about **$3.17 million in individual contributions** and about **$380,000 in presidential public funds**.

### Interpretation

This financial scale is orders of magnitude smaller than major Democratic super-PAC and nonprofit nodes. That narrows a specific hypothesis: major-party-scale donor capture cannot simply be imported without evidence. It does not establish clean governance, wise judgment, or moral goodness.

### High-value Green questions

- donor concentration within the comparatively small campaign;
- large vendor / consultant relationships;
- internal party governance and candidate-selection quality;
- ballot-access disputes and litigation;
- foreign-policy relationships and judgment;
- strategic claims versus measurable electoral / movement outcomes;
- whether public/small-dollar funding changes accountability behavior in ways relevant to a specific capture hypothesis.

**Historical working weight:** low evidence in this dataset for major-party-scale financial capture; governance and strategic accountability remain open lanes.

## Probe E — Progressive movement finance / Working Families Party and allied infrastructure

The recovered investigation records Working Families Party PAC at about **$17.6 million in receipts** from January 2025 through May 2026, with only about **$1.58 million** reported as contributions and most receipts in categories requiring source-level decomposition before interpretation.

This is a missingness node, not an invitation to guess.

### Candidate capture model

Progressive organizations can be materially dependent on wealthy donors, unions, foundations, nonprofit infrastructure, or executives whose preferences may obtain agenda-setting or veto power.

### Claim-specific countermodel

Funding from a wealthy donor does not itself establish capture. A direct test is whether the organization retains meaningful decision independence, including on issues materially adverse to the funder.

### Discriminators

- governance rights held by donors/funders;
- board composition;
- concentration of revenue;
- issue priorities before/after major funding changes;
- willingness to act against major-funder preferences when the scoped capture claim predicts compliance;
- rank-and-file/member influence relative to executives and funders.

## Cross-regime topology

The comparison is not `left = right with different donors`.

Historical candidate forms:

```text
CURRENT RIGHT
wealth / business ownership
    -> direct governing authority
    -> policy / state power
    -> private + coalition gain

DEMOCRATIC ESTABLISHMENT
wealth / organized donor networks
    -> PACs + nonprofits + lobbying + institutions
    -> candidate selection / agenda pressure
    -> policy / coalition behavior

PROGRESSIVE / LEFT INFRASTRUCTURE
foundations + unions + wealthy patrons + grassroots money
    -> movement organizations / nonprofits / media / candidates
    -> agenda + organizational capacity
    -> capture, autonomy, or mixed dependence

GREENS
small-dollar + public funding + low institutional access
    -> limited electoral infrastructure
    -> different capture mechanisms and capacity constraints
```

These are working models only. Crossovers are expected.

## Claim-specific exculpatory / falsification lane

**This replaces the legacy `Mandatory positive / exculpatory lane`.**

Do not inventory generic beneficial conduct.

Preserve only evidence that materially weakens or defeats the specific harm/capture claim being tested. Examples:

- a candidate demonstrably resists the donor network that a capture hypothesis predicts will control them;
- a union membership vote overrides leadership/funder preference, narrowing executive-capture attribution;
- a grantee acts materially against a funder's documented preferences, weakening a donor-control claim;
- a funding structure is shown to exist primarily because of documented retaliation risk, weakening an influence-concealment claim;
- a policy outcome occurs without the alleged donor/lobby pathway, supporting an independent-cause model;
- a supposed influence campaign repeatedly fails despite high spending, narrowing claims of control.

```text
claim-specific falsifier = required
unrelated good conduct = out of scope
```

## Method lock

Do not call an organization corrupt because donor identities are undisclosed.

Do not call donor anonymity harmless merely because it is legal.

Do not expose low-power individuals merely to satisfy abstract transparency when disclosure adds little to understanding consequential power and creates credible harm risk.

Do not protect billionaire-scale influence behind the language of privacy when identifying source-of-power is necessary for public accountability.

Do not treat Democratic, progressive, Green, socialist, labor, Palestinian-rights, nonprofit, or activist organizations as interchangeable.

Do not assume anti-oligarchy rhetoric proves independence from oligarchic funding.

Do not assume receiving money from a wealthy donor proves capture.

Do not require generic positive conduct to counterbalance harmful or captured conduct.

Preserve the question that matters:

> Who can alter whose behavior, through what resource or constraint, with what observable result?
