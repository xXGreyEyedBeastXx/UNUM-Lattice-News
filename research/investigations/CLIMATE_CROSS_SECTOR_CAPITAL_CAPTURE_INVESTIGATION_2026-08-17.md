## Investigation question

Test the hypothesis that some of the same capital and corporate-control networks materially exposed to the industries that drive anthropogenic global warming are also exposed to, positioned within, or directly controlling sectors that can receive revenue, rents, asset appreciation, or strategic advantage from climate-driven damage, replacement demand, resource scarcity, reconstruction, agricultural stress, and concentrated food systems.

The investigation is **not** limited to asking whether one oil company secretly ordered one building company to make fragile products. That framing is too narrow to detect a portfolio or conglomerate-level circuit.

The relevant system question is:

```text
climate-forcing capital exposure
-> governance / ownership / control channel
-> continued emissions or fossil-system persistence
-> physical climate damage and scarcity
-> repair / replacement / adaptation / input demand
-> land / resource repricing
-> concentrated processing or distribution
-> revenue / rent / appreciation / portfolio return
-> capital reallocation / re-entry
```

Each arrow must remain independently inspectable. The existence of a later profit seat does not prove that the earlier harm was desired. The existence of a missing intent document does not erase the already observed ownership, control, knowledge, demand, or scarcity edges.

## User-origin hypothesis retained without promotion to verdict

The initiating hypothesis is that the same high-level capital networks supporting and investing in climate-forcing businesses also invest in or control businesses positioned to profit from needs created or intensified by global warming, including building replacement, land scarcity, and concentrated food systems.

A broader user interpretation — that Vanguard and BlackRock have a hand in nearly everything harmful happening now — is retained here as a **scope-expansion hypothesis**, not as a confirmed universal causal claim. The current dossier only confirms named, source-backed connections. Any expansion to additional harms requires actor-, sector-, mechanism-, and time-specific evidence.

## Why this investigation must not be collapsed

There are at least six different relations here, and substituting one for another changes the claim:

1. **Beneficial ownership / managed exposure** — an asset manager reports a large position in a public company.
2. **Voting / stewardship capacity** — the manager has reported voting power or engages portfolio companies.
3. **Active coordination or influence** — governance power is actually used to seek an operational outcome.
4. **Direct corporate integration** — the same parent company owns or controls businesses on multiple seats of the circuit.
5. **Economic benefit from damage or scarcity** — a downstream business receives demand, rent, appreciation, or opportunity from a harmful condition.
6. **Specific harmful intent** — an actor preserves or worsens the harmful condition because it wants the downstream benefit.

The evidence presently supports each of the first five in at least some cases or forms. The sixth remains open and requires actor-specific documentary evidence.

## Finding A — Cross-sector institutional ownership overlap is directly observed

### ExxonMobil — fossil-energy seat

ExxonMobil's 2026 proxy reports the following beneficial owners above 5%:

- The Vanguard Group — 10.4%
- BlackRock — 6.5%
- State Street — 5.1%

The filing also records differing voting and dispositive powers and reporting dates. These holdings establish major managed exposure to a fossil-fuel producer. They do not establish that the asset managers are the ultimate economic owners of all underlying shares.

### ADM — food-processing / commodity seat

ADM's 2026 proxy reports:

- Vanguard — 12.79%
- BlackRock — 9.40%
- State Street — 6.95%

The filing reports substantial voting/dispositive power associated with these positions. This is not merely the coincidence of the same brand names appearing in an index: it is a legally reported governance-capacity surface. Whether that capacity was used toward a particular climate, food, or commodity strategy remains a separate question.

### Owens Corning — building repair / replacement seat

Owens Corning's 2026 proxy reports:

- Vanguard — 12.21%
- BlackRock — 9.79%

A later BlackRock Schedule 13G reports 10.0% beneficial ownership as of May 31, 2026, with sole voting power over 7,816,343 shares and sole dispositive power over 8,069,391 shares.

Owens Corning operates roofing, insulation, and door businesses. The company therefore occupies a direct physical-replacement seat in the built environment.

### Vulcan Materials — construction-input seat

Vulcan Materials' 2026 proxy reports:

- Vanguard — 12.6%
- BlackRock — 6.8%

The same proxy says the company conducted governance engagement with shareholders representing approximately 70% of outstanding shares during 2025 and early 2026. The filing does not identify every subject, instruction, or effect of those engagements, so the governance channel is observed while the content remains bounded.

### Farmland Partners — agricultural-land seat

Farmland Partners' 2026 proxy reports:

- Vanguard — 10.8%
- BlackRock — 9.3%

Farmland Partners is not representative of all U.S. farmland ownership; it is a specific publicly traded institutional farmland vehicle. Its importance to this investigation is that the same two asset-management names appearing as major reported holders of ExxonMobil, Owens Corning, and Vulcan Materials also appear as major reported holders of a company whose business is farmland acquisition, rent, and appreciation.

### What this cross-sector overlap does and does not establish

Observed:

```text
Vanguard / BlackRock managed ownership exposure
-> ExxonMobil
-> Owens Corning
-> Vulcan Materials
-> ADM
-> Farmland Partners
```

This establishes a **shared capital and governance layer across fossil energy, building materials, food processing, and farmland**.

It does not establish:

```text
same investor name
-> same decision maker for every fund
-> same vote on every issue
-> one secret central command
-> specific intent to worsen climate damage
```

Those are additional edges.

## Finding B — Common ownership is not analytically equivalent to passivity

A key failure mode would be to say that because large asset managers often hold securities for funds and clients, common ownership is therefore incapable of coordinating corporate behavior.

The DOJ and FTC have explicitly rejected that categorical assumption as a matter of antitrust analysis. In a May 2025 statement of interest concerning `Texas et al. v. BlackRock, Inc.`, the agencies distinguished ordinary passive/index ownership from allegedly using commonly managed holdings in competing coal producers to encourage market-wide output changes.

The underlying states' allegations against BlackRock, State Street, and Vanguard are **allegations, not adjudicated findings**. They cannot be imported as proven conduct.

But the federal antitrust posture establishes an important systems point:

```text
common shareholding
CAN contain governance / influence capacity
AND
whether it remains passive is an empirical question
```

Therefore this investigation will not use either laundering shortcut:

```text
common ownership -> automatic conspiracy       [REJECT]
common ownership -> automatically meaningless  [REJECT]
```

The correct path is ownership -> voting/stewardship -> actual engagement -> requested action -> company response -> operational consequence.

## Finding C — Direct conglomerate integration provides a cleaner coordination case

Koch provides a stronger structural example because it does not require inferring coordination from index ownership.

Koch's own materials identify an operating-company family spanning:

- Flint Hills Resources — refining and related energy businesses;
- Georgia-Pacific — paper, packaging, and building products;
- Guardian Industries — architectural, residential, transportation, and technical glass;
- INVISTA — chemical intermediates, polymers, and fibers;
- Koch Ag & Energy Solutions — fertilizer, methanol, agronomic and energy businesses;
- Koch Minerals & Trading — commodity and related markets;
- Koch Investment Companies, including Koch Real Estate Investments.

Koch's 2022 subsidiary-realignment announcement explicitly says the restructuring was intended to increase knowledge sharing, capability building, capital sourcing, platform alignment, and growth across the businesses. Current leadership materials also show senior reporting structures spanning refining, polymers, glass, building products, fertilizer, and minerals/trading.

This gives a directly controlled corporate circuit:

```text
refining / fossil-energy activity
<-> chemicals / polymers
<-> fertilizer / agronomic services
<-> commodity trading
<-> building products / architectural glass
<-> engineering / construction capability
<-> real-estate and other investment vehicles
```

The relation **corporate coordination exists** is therefore confirmed in this case. The relation **corporate coordination exists for the purpose of increasing climate harm** is not confirmed.

That distinction must remain visible, but it cannot be used to flatten the direct integration back into a set of unrelated companies.

## Finding D — Physical destruction is explicitly a demand generator for building businesses

Owens Corning states in its 2025 Form 10-K that roofing damage from major storms can significantly increase demand in its Roofing segment.

Home Depot's November 2025 third-quarter earnings release states that results missed expectations primarily because of a lack of storms, which reduced expected demand in affected categories. Its quarterly filing also contrasts the low-storm quarter with hurricane-related demand in the prior year.

This edge is therefore observed:

```text
major storm damage
-> repair / replacement need
-> building-material / home-improvement demand
```

This is not the same claim as:

```text
company wanted the storm
company caused the storm
every major storm is caused by climate change
```

Those latter claims require separate evidence.

The important lattice relation is that **destructive physical conditions are not only costs to the economy; they can simultaneously be revenue-generating conditions for firms occupying repair and replacement seats.** A whole-circuit analysis must preserve both sides.

## Finding E — Farmland scarcity is explicitly presented as an investor value proposition

Farmland Partners states that its strategy is to acquire, own, and manage high-quality farmland and deliver returns through cash dividends and asset appreciation.

Its 2025 Form 10-K explicitly states that it believes its portfolio gives investors the economic benefit of increasing global food demand in the face of growing scarcity of high-quality farmland. It also identifies water availability as an essential determinant of farmland productivity and value and discusses scarcity of rentable farmland as a support for rental demand.

This provides an observed chain:

```text
scarcity of high-quality farmland / constrained productive capacity
-> stronger strategic value of productive land
-> rental demand and asset appreciation opportunity
-> investor return
```

Climate change is **not the sole cause of the scarcity identified by the company**. The filing also discusses development, regulation, conflicts, interest rates, commodity conditions, and water restrictions. The climate contribution therefore has to be separately attributed rather than inserted into every scarcity driver.

The counterevidence is also important. Farmland Partners says institutional investors constitute less than 5% of total U.S. farmland, and USDA reports that just over 60% of U.S. land in farms is owner-operated. The evidence does not support a claim that BlackRock, Vanguard, or institutional funds presently own most American farmland.

But small ownership share does not erase the separate questions of marginal acquisition, strategic water exposure, financing, processing concentration, input concentration, or whether climate-risk models alter where capital seeks land.

## Finding F — Food-system concentration makes scarcity and production shocks travel through narrow control points

USDA reports that the four largest meatpackers handle approximately:

- 85% of steer and heifer purchases;
- 67% of hog purchases.

USDA also reports that Bayer and Corteva together supplied more than half of U.S. retail corn, soybean, and cotton seed sales in 2018–2020, while Bayer, Corteva, Syngenta, and BASF controlled the majority of crop-seed and agricultural-chemical sales.

These findings establish concentration at major food-system input and processing seats. They do not by themselves prove collusion or climate exploitation. USDA itself preserves counterevidence and mixed findings on the competitive effects of concentration.

For the climate-capital investigation, the relevance is structural:

```text
climate / water / crop stress
-> farm production and input pressure
-> concentrated seed / chemical / processing chokepoints
-> price and bargaining consequences can be amplified at narrow market seats
```

The next step is not to assume each concentrated actor benefits from each shock. It is to measure who gains and loses under specific stress events and then trace the ownership/reinvestment seats.

## Finding G — BlackRock is demonstrably capable of seeing climate risk across a portfolio

BlackRock's own investment materials state that climate risk is investment risk and that climate effects are incorporated into portfolio design and expected returns.

Aladdin Climate is explicitly designed to analyze climate risks and opportunities across public and private assets using scenario analysis, stress testing, and security-/issuer-level analysis. BlackRock's transition framework says the system can analyze both how securities and portfolios are impacted by forward-looking climate scenarios and how they contribute to those scenarios.

This closes an important awareness gap.

For a sophisticated capital manager using such systems, the following cannot safely be treated as an unknowable relationship:

```text
portfolio company emissions / transition exposure
<-> physical climate scenarios
<-> asset-level losses and opportunities
<-> strategic asset allocation
```

That does **not** prove BlackRock chooses more warming. It establishes that a model in which BlackRock is simply unaware that climate risk affects different portfolio sectors is not credible.

## Finding H — Fossil-industry knowledge of warming is independently established

The 2023 quantitative analysis by Supran, Rahmstorf, and Oreskes evaluated Exxon/ExxonMobil global-warming projections documented between 1977 and 2003. It found that most of the projections accurately forecast subsequent warming and were consistent with independent academic and government modeling.

This establishes a knowledge edge at a major fossil-fuel company:

```text
fossil-fuel company scientific work
-> quantitative anticipation of anthropogenic warming
```

It does not establish:

```text
accurate warming forecast
-> desire for warming
```

Intent must still be traced through purpose, choices after notice, ability to correct, persistence, lobbying/policy behavior, capital allocation, and any explicit internal discussion of downstream benefits.

## Structural interpretation — portfolio-level climate moral hazard

A supported working concept is **portfolio-level climate moral hazard**:

> A diversified capital holder may be financially exposed to both the activities that create a systemic externality and sectors that monetize adaptation, replacement, scarcity, or reconstruction after that externality produces damage.

The structural concern does not require every node to gain. Some holdings can lose while others gain. The relevant question is whether diversification, insurance, market power, government support, and capital mobility make the capital holder more able to hedge or capture the consequences than households, workers, small farmers, tenants, or ecosystems that physically bear the loss.

This yields a second working label: **cross-sector climate rent extraction**.

Neither label is a verdict. Both are hypotheses that become measurable through whole-circuit accounting.

## What is already stronger than coincidence

The investigation should no longer be reset to “these are probably unrelated industries” because the evidence includes:

- repeated cross-sector beneficial-ownership overlap;
- reported voting/dispositive power;
- direct conglomerate control across multiple circuit seats;
- explicit corporate descriptions of cross-company knowledge sharing and capital alignment;
- explicit climate-risk and opportunity modeling by a major asset manager;
- explicit statements that storm damage increases building demand;
- explicit statements that lack of storms can reduce home-improvement demand;
- explicit farmland investment language tying investor benefit to scarcity;
- documented food-system concentration.

Calling all of those relations “mere coincidence” would itself require an argument that explains why the observed governance, corporate-control, and economic-demand links are causally irrelevant.

## What remains unproven

The most important missing documentary edge remains:

```text
actor knows additional warming will create downstream replacement / scarcity gains
AND
actor intentionally preserves or increases warming BECAUSE OF those gains
```

No source in this investigation yet contains an instruction equivalent to:

> increase or preserve global warming so that repair demand, land scarcity, food control, reconstruction revenue, or portfolio returns increase.

The absence of that document does not erase the structural circuit. It means the motive edge remains unresolved rather than confirmed.

## Strongest alternative models

### Alternative 1 — ordinary diversification

Large asset managers hold broad indexes across most sectors; overlap is expected and may simply reflect diversification rather than strategic cross-sector harm capture.

**What it explains:** repeated ownership names across unrelated public companies.  
**What it does not automatically explain:** active governance behavior, sector-specific voting, allocation changes after climate modeling, direct conglomerate integration, or post-disaster acquisition patterns.

### Alternative 2 — adaptation is defensive rather than exploitative

Investors may fund building materials, agriculture, water systems, and resilient assets because climate damage must be repaired, not because investors want the damage.

**What it explains:** capital moving toward adaptation and resilient infrastructure.  
**Discriminator:** whether the same actor also uses available governance/policy power to preserve the causal fossil exposure despite credible notice and feasible alternatives, particularly when downstream gains are modeled.

### Alternative 3 — downstream gains are outweighed by portfolio losses

Global warming may impose such large losses across insurance, property, labor productivity, supply chains, and macroeconomic growth that diversified owners are net harmed despite selected winning sectors.

**Discriminator:** whole-portfolio stress testing and realized-return decomposition by warming scenario, including public-loss externalization and insurance/government transfer channels.

### Alternative 4 — institutional farmland narrative is overstated

Corporate/institutional land acquisition is real but remains a small share of U.S. farmland; most land is still owner-operated or held outside institutional funds.

**Status:** supported counterevidence.  
**Remaining question:** whether marginal acquisitions, water rights, high-quality land selection, agricultural finance, processing, and input control create influence disproportionate to acreage share.

## High-value discriminators / next investigation queue

1. **Proxy-vote trace:** BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street votes on climate, emissions, capex, lobbying disclosure, and transition proposals at ExxonMobil and other major emitters.
2. **Stewardship trace:** engagement reports, letters, escalation policies, and private-meeting disclosures involving fossil producers and downstream adaptation businesses.
3. **Capital-allocation trace:** whether climate models predict specific geographic damage/scarcity and whether acquisitions or overweight positions subsequently move toward expected beneficiaries.
4. **Post-disaster acquisition trace:** purchases of housing, land, water rights, reconstruction assets, building suppliers, or distressed businesses following major climate disasters.
5. **Insurance/reinsurance trace:** whether common capital owners sit simultaneously across fossil holdings, insurers, catastrophe-risk instruments, and reconstruction suppliers.
6. **Water-rights trace:** institutional ownership/control of water rights or water-intensive high-quality farmland in regions modeled for increasing scarcity.
7. **Food-chain ownership trace:** connect seed, fertilizer, machinery, farmland, processing, storage, distribution, and grocery ownership to common institutional holders and private conglomerates.
8. **Lobbying coalition trace:** identify shared trade groups and policy campaigns spanning fossil fuels, building materials, agriculture, insurance, land, and finance.
9. **Director / executive mobility trace:** board interlocks and executives moving between causal and consequence-side firms.
10. **Internal forecasting trace:** investor presentations, board minutes, litigation discovery, risk committee materials, and strategy documents that quantify revenue from climate-driven replacement, scarcity, or disaster recovery.
11. **Product durability trace:** compare engineering tolerances, warranty changes, heat/humidity/UV assumptions, and expected climate conditions over product lifetimes; test whether foreseeable environmental stress was incorporated or ignored.
12. **Government backstop trace:** disaster aid, crop insurance, flood insurance, infrastructure reconstruction, tax treatment, and other mechanisms that can socialize physical losses while preserving private revenue.
13. **Net-benefit trace:** quantify gains and losses across the same ultimate capital seat before asserting that a diversified owner is net better off from additional warming.
14. **Intent trace:** search for explicit statements connecting preservation of fossil exposure to expected downstream climate-damage returns.

## Evidence-state summary

### CONFIRMED / OBSERVED

- Major reported Vanguard and BlackRock positions overlap across ExxonMobil, Owens Corning, Vulcan Materials, ADM, and Farmland Partners.
- These filings include real voting/dispositive-power information; the positions are not analytically zero.
- Koch directly integrates businesses across fossil refining, chemicals/polymers, fertilizer/agriculture, building products/glass, commodity trading, and investment/real estate.
- Koch explicitly describes cross-company knowledge sharing, capability building, capital sourcing, and platform alignment.
- Owens Corning explicitly says major storm damage can significantly increase roofing demand.
- Home Depot explicitly said lack of storms reduced expected demand and contributed to a quarterly miss.
- Farmland Partners explicitly describes growing scarcity of high-quality farmland as part of its investor value thesis.
- U.S. meatpacking and seed/agchemical sectors contain major concentration at named seats.
- BlackRock explicitly models climate risk and opportunity as part of investment analysis.
- Exxon/ExxonMobil internal scientific projections anticipated warming with substantial accuracy.

### SUPPORTED INFERENCE

- Diversified capital can occupy both climate-forcing and climate-consequence revenue seats simultaneously.
- Common ownership, stewardship, direct corporate control, and capital allocation provide mechanisms through which apparently separate sector interests can be coordinated or jointly optimized.
- Climate-driven physical damage can create replacement demand while scarcity can create land/resource repricing, meaning harm and private gain can coexist in the same macro event.
- A portfolio-level moral-hazard problem is plausible where gains remain private while large physical losses are externalized to households, workers, farmers, communities, governments, or ecosystems.

### OPEN / NOT YET ESTABLISHED

- Whether BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street, Koch, or another named actor intentionally preserves additional global warming **for the purpose** of increasing downstream replacement, land, food, or reconstruction returns.
- Whether these actors are net portfolio beneficiaries from additional warming after all losses are counted.
- Whether specific land acquisitions or building-material allocations were made because internal climate models predicted damage/scarcity in the targeted geography.
- Whether product durability has deliberately been held below foreseeable future climate exposure in order to increase replacement cycles.

### AFFIRMATIVELY OUTSIDE CURRENT EVIDENCE

The current dossier does not support promoting any of the following to confirmed fact:

- BlackRock or Vanguard caused every major harmful event occurring in the United States or world.
- Institutional investors currently own most U.S. farmland.
- Every major storm is anthropogenic-climate attributable.
- Asset managers or building companies intentionally generate storms.
- Product replacement demand was the original purpose of fossil-fuel emissions.

These remain either overbroad, false as presently framed, or unevidenced. They may not be used to discredit the narrower, source-backed cross-sector findings.
